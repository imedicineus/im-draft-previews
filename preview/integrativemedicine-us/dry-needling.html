Dry Needling

Dry needling is the most orthopedic thing I do. It is aimed squarely at the structures that move you: muscles, tendons, ligaments and the places where they anchor to bone. If you have a knot that comes back every week or a tendon that complains every time you train, this is the page for you.

The Short Version

Dry needling uses the same thin, sterile, single use needles as acupuncture. "Dry" simply means nothing is injected. The needle itself is the treatment. I place it into trigger points, tight bands, motor points and painful soft tissue, and sometimes near bone where a tendon or ligament attaches.

It is often called orthopedic acupuncture, and in my hands it is part of a licensed acupuncture practice. I am certified in dry needling in addition to my doctorate in acupuncture and Oriental medicine.

Where It Tends to Help

People most often come to us for:

  • Neck and upper back tightness, including desk posture strain
  • Low back pain and hip tightness
  • Shoulder problems, including rotator cuff irritation
  • Tennis and golfer's elbow
  • Knee, calf, shin and plantar foot pain
  • Sports strains and overuse injuries
  • Tension headaches linked to tight neck and jaw muscles

Research suggests dry needling may help reduce myofascial pain and improve range of motion for some people. Results vary, and I will tell you honestly what I think it can do for your situation.

What to Expect

  1. Assessment. I check posture, movement, strength and where tissue is guarding.
  2. Needling. You may feel a quick local twitch when the needle reaches an active trigger point. That twitch is a good sign and passes in a moment.
  3. Integration. I often combine dry needling with classical acupuncture, cupping, gua sha or electrical stimulation.
  4. Homework. Stretches and movement drills so the change sticks.

Mild soreness for a day or two is common, much like after a hard workout. Drink water, keep moving gently and use heat if you like.

What Is a Trigger Point?

A trigger point is a hyperirritable spot in a taut band of muscle. It can be tender to touch and can refer pain elsewhere, which is why a knot in the shoulder blade can feel like a headache or arm ache. Trigger points may form after injury, overuse, poor posture or stress.

The Twitch Response

When a needle reaches an active trigger point, the muscle often produces a local twitch. Research suggests this response is associated with the band releasing and with changes in the local chemical environment that sustains pain. Not every treatment needs a twitch, but it tells me I found the right spot.

Why the Body Responds

The needle creates a tiny controlled micro injury. Your body responds with increased local blood flow and its own repair signaling. Tissues such as tendons and ligaments, which have a limited blood supply, may benefit from that renewed attention. Needling also sends new signals through the nervous system, which may help calm pain processing, an idea related to the gate control theory proposed by Melzack and Wall in 1965.

Safety

Needles are sterile, single use and disposed of after each treatment. Proper anatomy training matters, which is why dry needling should be done by a licensed, trained practitioner. Tell me if you are pregnant, take blood thinners or have a bleeding disorder.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does dry needling hurt?

The insertion is quick. The twitch can feel like a brief cramp, then the muscle often relaxes.

How soon will I notice a change?

Some people feel looser right away. Others notice change over several sessions.

Can I work out afterward?

Light movement is good. Save a heavy session for the next day.
