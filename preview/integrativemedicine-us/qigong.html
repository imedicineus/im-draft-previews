What Qigong Is

"Qi" is often translated as vital energy and "gong" as skill or work cultivated over time. Medical qigong is the branch of the tradition focused on health. A session might include standing postures, flowing movements, self massage and breathing patterns, all done at a pace almost anyone can follow.

Qigong is not a religion, and you do not need to adopt any belief system to practice it. People of every faith and none practice in our classes.

Two Ways to Experience It

External qigong is done for you. In the clinic, I may use qigong techniques along with acupuncture to help you relax and settle.

Internal qigong is done by you. It is the daily practice I teach patients, and it is where most of the long term value lives. Ten calm minutes a day can matter more than one long session a month.

03

The 70 Percent Principle

One rule I teach early: work at about 70 percent of your capacity. Never push to strain, never force a stretch, never hold your breath to finish a movement. Qigong builds slowly, and pushing too hard tends to create tension, which is the opposite of what we want. If a movement does not feel right for your body that day, make it smaller.

04

A Short Daily Routine

I teach a short routine I call Nine Celestial Cycle qigong that takes about nine minutes. It moves through the body from head to feet with breath and gentle motion. Patients often do it first thing in the morning or before bed. In class and in clinic, I will walk you through each movement so you can practice safely at home.

What Research Suggests

Research on qigong and its close cousin tai chi has grown considerably. A few places to start reading:

  • A Cochrane review looked at tai chi and qigong for several conditions (PMC6360922)
  • Research on qigong and stress and mood (PubMed 30125554)
  • Reviews of mind body movement and health outcomes (PMC8504989, PMC5861462)
  • A review on qigong and quality of life (PMC3893407)
  • Further research on mind body practices (PMC8257957)
  • Overviews from Harvard Health and the National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health

Overall, studies suggest these practices may help with balance, stress, sleep and quality of life for many people. Study quality varies, and research continues.

Breath and the Nervous System

Slow, relaxed breathing is one of the most direct ways to influence the nervous system. Long, easy exhales tend to encourage the rest and digest response. In qigong, breath and movement are linked, so the practice trains this pattern without you having to think about it.

Balance and Movement

Many qigong movements shift weight slowly from one leg to the other, rotate the spine gently and coordinate arms with legs. That combination builds balance, body awareness and mobility. For older adults especially, balance training is valuable, and qigong offers it in a gentle form.

Qigong, Tai Chi and Martial Arts

Tai chi is a flowing martial art that shares roots with qigong. My own path ran through both. I have trained in martial arts for more than thirty five years, hold a 4th degree black belt in Lost Legacy (since 2004) and trained as a disciple at the Shaolin Temple. The health practices I teach today come from that background, simplified for everyday people.

How Qigong Fits a Treatment Plan

Qigong works well alongside acupuncture, herbal medicine and functional medicine. Treatment happens in the clinic. Qigong is what you do on the other six days of the week. Patients who practice regularly often tell me they feel more in charge of their own health.

Getting Started Safely

  • Talk with your doctor before starting any new exercise program, especially with heart, balance or joint concerns
  • Start with short sessions
  • Use a chair for support if needed
  • Stay at 70 percent effort

What a Class Looks Like

A typical class opens with gentle loosening of the joints from neck to ankles. Then we move into standing postures and flowing sequences, linking each movement with slow breath. We finish with quiet standing or seated stillness and self massage. Beginners stand in the back, watch and follow at their own pace. There is no performance and no competition. Many people say the hardest part is simply slowing down.

Practicing at Home

Consistency matters more than length. Choose a time you can keep, such as after brushing your teeth in the morning. Practice in the same spot if possible, with room to move your arms. Wear comfortable clothes and flat shoes or bare feet. If you miss a day, simply start again the next day.

The Three Regulations

Classical teaching describes qigong as regulating three things together: the body, the breath and the mind. Regulating the body means relaxed, aligned posture: knees soft, spine long, shoulders dropped. Regulating the breath means slow, smooth, quiet breathing through the nose, without forcing it. Regulating the mind means gentle attention on the movement and the breath, letting distracting thoughts pass. Beginners often focus on one at a time. Over months of practice, the three start to happen together, and that is when many people say qigong begins to feel like moving meditation.

Qigong for Every Age

I have taught teenagers preparing for exams, busy parents, first responders, people recovering from illness and adults in their eighties. Qigong scales well. A young athlete may hold a stance longer and sink lower. An older adult may do the same movement seated with a smaller range. Both are practicing correctly, as long as they stay within the 70 percent principle.

Retreats and Deeper Study

For students who want to go further, I have led retreats including trips to the Shaolin Temple in China, where students practiced in the setting where many of these traditions developed. Deeper study builds on the same foundations: posture, breath, relaxation and steady daily practice.

A Simple Breathing Exercise

Stand or sit comfortably. Breathe in through the nose for a slow count of four, letting the belly expand. Breathe out through the nose for a slow count of six. Repeat for one or two minutes. If you feel lightheaded, return to normal breathing.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I need to be flexible?

No. Movements are adapted to your body.

Is this the same as yoga?

They share ideas about breath and awareness, but qigong is rooted in Chinese medicine and martial arts and is usually done standing and moving slowly.

How do I join a class?

Call us or see our events page for current classes.

What should I wear?

Loose, comfortable clothing and flat shoes.

Can I practice with a health condition?

Many people can, with modifications. Check with your doctor first and tell me about your condition.

How long until I notice anything?

Many people feel calmer after one session. Balance and stamina change gradually with regular practice.
