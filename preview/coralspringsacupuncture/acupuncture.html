Is Acupuncture Right for Me?

Most people find their way here for one of three reasons: something hurts and has not let up, stress and sleep have gotten out of hand, or a friend in town kept telling them to try it. Common reasons for a first visit include neck and back pain, sciatica, shoulder and knee trouble, headaches, digestive complaints, anxiety and restless sleep.

Acupuncture is not a fit for everything, and Dr. Fields will say so when it is not. It works alongside your physician's care rather than replacing it. The simplest way to find out is a conversation: call 954-775-3765 or request a free consultation, and he will listen to your story before recommending anything.

What Happens on My First Visit?

Plan for a longer first appointment. It runs in roughly this order:

  1. Your story. Dr. Fields asks about the main complaint and also about sleep, digestion, energy, stress, past injuries and medications. In Chinese medicine, those details shape the treatment.
  2. A two part exam. He checks range of motion, muscle tone and tender areas the way an orthopedic clinician would, then looks at your pulse and tongue the way a classical Chinese medicine practitioner would.
  3. Treatment. You rest comfortably on the table while fine needles are placed. Many people feel a dull heaviness or warmth, then sink into deep relaxation. Some fall asleep.
  4. The plan. Before you leave, you will know what Dr. Fields thinks is going on, what he suggests next and what you can do at home.

Which Techniques Might Be Used?

Dr. Fields chooses tools to suit the person on the table. Depending on your needs, treatment may include:

  • Classical body points along the meridians, plus points far from the painful area
  • Ear points and hand point systems
  • Needling into tight muscles, tendons and ligaments, including dry needling for trigger points
  • Gentle electrical stimulation on the needles
  • Moxibustion, the warming herb mugwort, or warmed needles
  • Far infrared heat or red light
  • Cupping or gua sha for broad areas of tension

Some patients add a custom Chinese herbal formula, and those with fatigue, hormone or digestive concerns may benefit from a functional medicine workup.

04

Does It Hurt?

Usually far less than people expect. The needles are slender, solid and flexible, nothing like the hollow needles used for shots or blood draws. You may feel a quick pinch, then a dull ache, tingling or warmth around the point. If anything feels sharp or uncomfortable, say so and Dr. Fields will adjust.

05

How Many Visits Will I Need?

There is no one size answer. A recent strain can respond quickly, while a problem that has been building for years usually needs a series of visits. Dr. Fields reviews progress with you regularly and will be honest if he thinks a different approach or a referral makes more sense.

06

What Can I Do at Home?

Treatment works best when you take part. Dr. Fields often sends patients home with stretches, breathing practices and simple food guidance from Chinese medicine. Some join his qigong classes, gentle movement with breath that he has taught for more than twenty years.

07

Can Acupuncture Help With Stress and Sleep?

Many Coral Springs patients juggle long commutes, kids' schedules and demanding jobs. When the nervous system stays in high alert mode, sleep, digestion and mood often suffer. Research suggests acupuncture may help encourage the rest and digest side of the nervous system, and many people report sleeping better in the days after a session. Dr. Fields often combines treatment with breathing practice so you have a tool to use at home between visits.

The deeper story

For readers who want the details: the research, the history and the reasoning behind how we work.

Why Would a Tiny Needle Do Anything?

Each needle makes a microscopic puncture. Your immune system notices it and responds the way it would to any small wound, sending blood flow, immune cells and repair signals to the area. In a joint or muscle that has been irritated for months, that fresh attention may be part of how acupuncture helps tissue move toward healing.

What About Inflammation?

Short term inflammation is part of repair. Long term inflammation keeps tissue sore. Studies suggest acupuncture can influence inflammatory signaling and may help nudge the body toward balance. Scientists are still mapping exactly how, and responses differ from person to person.

How Does It Affect Pain Signals?

In 1965, Ronald Melzack and Patrick Wall described the gate control theory, the idea that the spinal cord can turn pain signals up or down before they reach the brain (Science, 1965). It explains why rubbing a stubbed toe helps. Needling sends new, non painful input through the same pathways, which may help quiet pain signaling. Research also suggests acupuncture can prompt the body to release its own endorphins and other feel good chemistry such as serotonin, dopamine and oxytocin.

What Do Brain Scans Show?

Functional MRI studies have found that acupuncture is associated with changes in activity in parts of the brain linked to pain, mood and stress. That fits with what many patients notice on the table: a settled, calm feeling that can last after they leave.

Why Do Tendons and Ligaments Heal Slowly?

These tissues have a limited blood supply, which is part of why an old ankle sprain or a sore elbow can linger. Needling near them creates small, controlled signals that may help draw circulation and repair activity to areas the body has been neglecting.

What About Knots in the Muscle?

A muscle knot, or trigger point, is a tight band that can squeeze local blood vessels and refer pain elsewhere. A needle placed into it can produce a brief twitch, after which the muscle often softens.

What Questions Are Researchers Still Asking?

  • Connective tissue. Work led by Dr. Helene Langevin suggested that turning a needle winds connective tissue around it, sending mechanical signals to cells (FASEB Journal, 2001).
  • The interstitium. A 2018 paper described fluid filled spaces woven through the body's tissues (Scientific Reports, 2018). How this relates to classical meridians is still open.
  • Electrical properties. Collagen produces tiny charges under pressure, and some researchers wonder whether needling interacts with them.
  • Blood vessel signaling. Research suggests acupuncture may affect nitric oxide, which helps vessels relax.

The classical language of qi, yin and yang and the modern language of nerves and fascia are two ways of describing the same body. Dr. Fields uses both.

Is Acupuncture Safe?

When performed by a licensed practitioner with sterile, single use needles, serious problems are uncommon. Minor bruising or soreness can happen. Tell Dr. Fields if you are pregnant, take blood thinners, have a pacemaker or a bleeding disorder.

Can Kids or Older Adults Have Acupuncture?

Often yes, with adjustments. Dr. Fields uses fewer needles, gentler stimulation and shorter sessions for people who are sensitive, and he may use non needle options such as pressure or warming techniques.
