Qigong at a Glance

  • What it is: slow, coordinated movement paired with relaxed breathing and calm attention
  • Where it comes from: Chinese medicine and the martial arts
  • Who can do it: almost anyone, standing or seated
  • What you need: comfortable clothes, a little space and about ten minutes
  • What it is not: a religion. No beliefs required.

The word "qi" is often translated as life energy, and "gong" as skill earned through practice. Medical qigong is the health focused branch of the tradition.

Two Sides of Qigong

In the treatment room, Dr. Fields may apply external qigong, techniques he performs to help you settle, often combined with acupuncture. At home, you practice internal qigong yourself. The second is where the long term benefits build, because a short daily habit adds up faster than an occasional long session.

03

Rule Number One: 70 Percent

Dr. Fields asks every student to stay at roughly 70 percent of their effort. Do not strain, do not force range of motion and never hold your breath to complete a movement. Qigong rewards patience. Pushing creates tension, and releasing tension is the whole point. If something feels wrong, make the movement smaller or rest.

A Nine Minute Habit

Dr. Fields teaches a compact routine he calls Nine Celestial Cycle qigong. It takes about nine minutes and moves from head to toe, linking each gentle motion to the breath. Many Coral Springs patients practice it in the morning before work or in the evening to wind down. He teaches it step by step so you can practice safely on your own.

The deeper story

For readers who want the details: the research, the history and the reasoning behind how we work.

Body, Breath, Mind

Classical teachers describe qigong as training three things at once. The body learns relaxed alignment: soft knees, long spine, heavy shoulders. The breath becomes slow, smooth and quiet through the nose. The mind rests gently on the movement and lets distractions pass. Beginners usually focus on one at a time, and over months the three begin to merge into what many describe as moving meditation.

The Breath Connection

Slow breathing with long, easy exhales is one of the most direct ways to influence the nervous system, and it tends to encourage the calmer rest and digest state. Qigong links every movement to the breath, so you practice this pattern without having to think about it.

Balance in Motion

Many qigong movements shift weight slowly between legs while the spine turns gently and the arms coordinate with the legs. That trains balance, coordination and body awareness. For older adults, gentle balance practice is especially valuable.

What the Research Says

Research on qigong and tai chi has expanded considerably. A Cochrane review examined these practices for several conditions (PMC6360922). Other studies have looked at stress, mood and quality of life (PubMed 30125554, PMC3893407), and reviews have examined mind body movement more broadly (PMC8504989, PMC5861462, PMC8257957). The NCCIH and Harvard Health publish accessible overviews. Overall, studies suggest these practices may support balance, stress management, sleep and quality of life, though study quality varies.

Martial Roots

Tai chi is a slow martial art closely related to qigong. Dr. Fields came to both through more than thirty five years of martial arts training. He has held a 4th degree black belt in Lost Legacy since 2004 and trained as a disciple at the Shaolin Temple. What he teaches today distills that background into something anyone can use.

Qigong in a Treatment Plan

Clinic visits happen once a week or less. Qigong covers the other days. Paired with acupuncture, herbal medicine or functional medicine, it gives patients something active to do for their own health, and many say it helps them feel more in control.

Starting Safely

  • Check with your physician before beginning, especially with heart, balance or joint concerns
  • Begin with a few minutes
  • Keep a chair nearby for support
  • Stay at 70 percent

A Two Minute Starter

Stand with feet hip width apart and knees slightly soft. Let your arms float up in front of you as you breathe in through the nose, then let them sink as you breathe out slowly. Repeat ten times, keeping shoulders relaxed. If you feel dizzy, stop and breathe normally.

A Typical Class, Start to Finish

Class opens with joint loosening from the neck down to the ankles, a few minutes of easy circles and shakes that warm the body. Next come standing postures, held softly while the breath slows. Then the flowing sequences: arms rising and sinking, weight shifting side to side, the spine turning gently. Each motion is matched to an inhale or an exhale. Class closes with quiet stillness and self massage of the face, arms and lower back. Newcomers stand toward the back and follow at their own pace. Nobody is graded, and nobody is watching you.

Making It a Habit

Tie practice to something you already do every day, such as your morning coffee or the end of the school pickup. Use the same spot so your body associates it with slowing down. Wear loose clothing and flat shoes, or go barefoot. Missed a day? Begin again tomorrow without guilt. Consistency over months matters more than any single session.

Qigong for Every Generation

Teens studying for exams, parents juggling schedules, first responders, people recovering from illness and adults in their eighties have all practiced with Dr. Fields. An athlete may sink lower and hold longer. An older adult may move seated with smaller range. Both are practicing correctly as long as they respect the 70 percent rule.

Why Slow Works

Our days are built for speed: commutes on Sample Road and University Drive, phones buzzing, schedules stacked back to back. Slow movement is a deliberate counterweight. Moving slowly makes you notice where you hold tension, how you shift weight and when your breath gets short. That awareness is a skill, and like any skill it improves with repetition. Many students say the biggest change is not in the movements themselves but in noticing stress earlier during the day and having a simple way to respond to it, such as a few slow breaths at a red light or a minute of standing practice before a meeting.

Practicing Outdoors in Florida

Morning and early evening are the most comfortable times. Pick shade, bring water and move indoors when lightning is nearby.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I need experience?

None at all. Beginners are welcome.

How is qigong different from yoga?

Both use breath and awareness. Qigong comes from Chinese medicine and martial arts and usually involves slow, standing movement rather than held floor postures.

Can I practice with arthritis or after surgery?

Often with modifications. Get your doctor's clearance first and tell Dr. Fields about your condition.
