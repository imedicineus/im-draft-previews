Dry Needling in Plain English

A thin, sterile, single use needle is placed into a trigger point or a tight band of muscle. Nothing is injected, which is where the word "dry" comes from. The aim is to help that tissue release and to restore more comfortable movement. Dr. Fields also needles tendons, ligaments and motor points when the exam points there.

Because it concentrates on the muscles and connective tissue that move your body, dry needling is often described as orthopedic acupuncture. Dr. Fields is a licensed acupuncturist with a doctorate in acupuncture and Oriental medicine, and he is certified in dry needling.

A Typical Session

Dr. Fields starts by watching how you move and pressing along the tissue to find active trigger points. When the needle reaches one, you may feel a brief twitch, a quick involuntary contraction that usually signals the right spot. He often combines needling with classical acupuncture, cupping, gua sha or electrical stimulation, then finishes with stretches you can repeat at home.

Expect some soreness for a day or two, similar to a hard workout. Stay hydrated, keep moving gently and use heat if it feels good.

Dry Needling Versus Classical Acupuncture

Think of the two as different maps of the same territory. Dry needling reads the body like a sports medicine clinician: which muscle is shortened, which band is irritable, which tendon is overloaded. The needle goes where the tissue is guilty, and the sensation is usually a twitch or a deep ache. Classical acupuncture reads the body through channels and patterns, choosing points that may sit far from the sore spot, and the sensation is more often heaviness, warmth or a spreading tingle. One is local and mechanical. The other is whole body and regulatory.

In practice, Dr. Fields blends the two. Dry needling addresses the tissue, and classical points address the person.

Understanding Trigger Points

Trigger points are tender, irritable spots in tight bands of muscle. They often refer pain to other areas, so a knot near the shoulder blade may show up as a headache or an aching arm. Injury, repetitive movement, posture and stress can all contribute.

Why the Twitch Matters

The local twitch response is a reflex contraction of the taut band. Research suggests it is associated with the band relaxing and with changes in the local chemistry that keeps pain going. Not every session needs a twitch, but it confirms the needle found active tissue.

The Biology After the Needle Comes Out

Once the needle is withdrawn, the local tissue gets busy. Circulation to the spot picks up, and the body begins its usual tidy up and rebuild routine. That is useful in places like the Achilles or the elbow, where tendons are poorly supplied with blood and tend to heal slowly. On the nervous system side, a burst of new sensory input can change how loudly the spinal cord relays pain, a concept first laid out in the 1965 gate control paper by Melzack and Wall.

Pairing Needling With Movement

A released muscle is an opportunity. Dr. Fields often follows needling with mobility drills or light strengthening, because new range of motion tends to stick when you use it. For runners that may be calf and foot work, and for desk workers it may be chest openers and upper back activation.

Safety First

Needles are sterile and single use. Anatomy training is essential, which is why dry needling should be performed by a licensed, trained practitioner. Let Dr. Fields know if you are pregnant, take blood thinners or have a bleeding disorder.
