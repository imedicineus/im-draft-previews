Trigger Points Explained

A myofascial trigger point is a hyperirritable spot within a taut band of muscle. Pressing it is tender and may reproduce referred pain in a predictable pattern. Trigger points are associated with overuse, postural strain, injury and stress, and they can limit range of motion and strength.

03

How It Works

Palpation. Dr. Fields locates trigger points by touch and movement testing.

Needling. A filament needle enters the trigger point. A brief local twitch response is common.

Reassessment. Range of motion and pain are rechecked immediately.

Loading. Targeted exercise helps the change last.

04

Evidence Summary

Systematic reviews have reported that dry needling can reduce pain in the short term for several musculoskeletal conditions, particularly neck, shoulder and low back pain, with results often similar to or better than comparison treatments. Evidence quality is mixed and long term data are limited, which is why we combine needling with active rehabilitation. Proposed mechanisms include local changes in muscle tone and blood flow and modulation of pain signaling, consistent with frameworks such as gate control theory.

Dry Needling and Acupuncture

Both use the same needles. Dry needling is guided by musculoskeletal anatomy and trigger point findings. Acupuncture uses Chinese medicine diagnosis and may treat areas far from the pain. Because Dr. Fields is trained in both, he can choose or combine approaches.

Athletes and Active Adults

Runners, golfers, tennis and pickleball players and gym goers often seek dry needling for tight hips, calves, shoulders and forearms. Treatment is timed around training and competition so soreness does not interfere.

What You May Feel

A twitch, a deep ache or a brief reproduction of your familiar pain. Afterward, soreness similar to a workout for a day or two is common.

Safety

Risks include bruising, soreness and, rarely, more serious complications such as pneumothorax when treating near the chest wall. Careful technique and anatomical knowledge reduce risk. Tell us about blood thinners, pregnancy, implants or a fear of needles.

A Typical Course

Visit one. Assessment, needling of the primary trigger points and a home exercise or two.

Visits two and three. Reassessment, needling as needed and progression of exercises.

Visits four to six. Less needling, more loading, and a plan for staying well.

Common Areas Treated

Upper trapezius and levator scapulae. Associated with neck pain and tension headache, often from desk work and phone use.

Gluteus medius and piriformis. Associated with hip and buttock pain and pain that travels down the leg.

Forearm extensors. Associated with tennis elbow.

Calf muscles. Associated with cramping, Achilles tightness and heel pain.

Quadratus lumborum. Associated with one sided low back pain.

Desk Work and Posture

Long hours at a screen are a common contributor to neck and shoulder trigger points. We suggest screen height adjustments, movement breaks every half hour and a few simple exercises such as chin tucks and shoulder blade retraction.
