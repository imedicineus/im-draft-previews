The deeper story

For readers who want the details: the research, the history and the reasoning behind how we work.

The Systems View

Conventional care is organized by specialty: cardiology, gastroenterology, endocrinology. That structure is excellent for acute illness and complex disease. For chronic, cross system complaints, though, it can leave gaps. Functional medicine looks at the connections. For example, poor sleep can raise cortisol, which can affect blood sugar, which can affect energy and mood. Digestive inflammation can affect nutrient absorption, which can affect thyroid hormone production. Following those threads is the heart of the work.

Reading Labs Differently

Standard reference ranges are built from population averages and are designed mainly to flag disease. Functional medicine practitioners often look at narrower optimal ranges and at patterns across several markers, such as how a thyroid panel lines up with iron, vitamin D and inflammatory markers. These interpretations are clinical judgments rather than diagnostic rules, and Dr. Fields explains his reasoning so you can discuss it with your doctor.

Common Focus Areas

Thyroid. A full thyroid panel can give more context than TSH alone. Antibody testing can identify autoimmune thyroiditis.

Gut health. Digestion affects nutrient status, immunity and inflammation. Food sensitivities, low stomach acid and imbalanced gut bacteria are common areas of investigation.

Hormones. Stress, sleep, body composition and age shape sex hormone and adrenal patterns.

Blood sugar. Fasting glucose, insulin and hemoglobin A1c offer a picture of metabolic health.

Iron and anemia. Recurring anemia often has a reason, such as absorption issues or blood loss, that deserves a closer look with your physician.

Lifestyle as Treatment

No supplement can outwork a lifestyle that keeps creating the problem. Plans usually include food changes, a consistent sleep window, stress practices such as qigong, movement and sometimes environmental changes at home or work.

Where Chinese Medicine Fits

Dr. Fields often blends functional medicine with acupuncture and Chinese herbal medicine. Labs show what is happening in measurable terms, and Chinese medicine offers a framework for the whole person pattern. Used together, they offer two complementary views.

Evidence and Limits

Functional medicine is a broad approach rather than a single treatment, so research tends to study specific parts: dietary changes, nutrient repletion, sleep and stress interventions. Some approaches have solid support and others are still being studied. Dr. Fields will tell you when something is well supported and when it is a reasonable trial.

A Sample Timeline

To show what working together looks like, here is a typical structure. Week one is the intake and lab orders. Week two or three is the report of findings and first plan. Over the next six to twelve weeks, follow ups focus on how the changes are going, with adjustments to supplements, herbs or acupuncture. Around the three month mark, labs are often repeated to compare against baseline. From there, the plan shifts toward maintenance and fewer visits. Every case differs, but this rhythm gives you a sense of the commitment.

Food First

Most plans start with food because it touches every system. Dr. Fields may suggest a period of removing common irritants, adding more vegetables and fiber, balancing protein at breakfast or adjusting meal timing. Restaurant heavy schedules are common in Boca Raton, so he offers strategies for ordering well at business lunches and dinners rather than demanding home cooking every night.

Sleep and Recovery

Poor sleep affects appetite hormones, blood sugar, mood and pain sensitivity. A consistent sleep schedule, a cool and dark room and a buffer between screens and bedtime are basic but powerful. When sleep is still difficult, acupuncture, herbs or targeted supplements may be added.

Stress Physiology

Chronic stress can affect digestion, hormones, immunity and sleep. Functional medicine looks at stress as a physical input, not only an emotional one. Breathing practices, qigong, time outdoors and setting boundaries around work hours are part of the conversation.

Supplements With a Purpose

Supplements are used to correct identified deficiencies or support specific goals, then reduced when they are no longer needed. Dr. Fields reviews what you already take, because many patients arrive with a cabinet full of products that duplicate each other. Quality matters, so he favors brands that publish third party testing.

Coordinating With Your Doctors

Your primary care physician, endocrinologist, gastroenterologist or gynecologist remains central to your care. With your permission, Dr. Fields shares findings so everyone is working from the same information. If anything suggests a condition requiring urgent medical attention, he will refer you promptly.

Is This Approach Right for You?

Functional medicine suits people who want to understand their health and are willing to make changes. It asks for participation: tracking symptoms, adjusting meals, protecting sleep and following through on testing. If you prefer a quick fix, it may feel slow. If you want a clear explanation and a structured path, it can be a good fit. The free consultation is the place to decide together whether to proceed.

What to Bring to the First Visit

Bring recent lab reports, a list of every medication and supplement with doses, a short timeline of major health events and a food log from three typical days, including one weekend day. The more complete the picture, the more useful the first session.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is functional medicine covered by insurance?

Coverage varies. Some lab work may be billable through your insurance. Call us to discuss.

Do I need to stop my medications?

No. Never stop prescribed medication without your prescriber. Functional medicine is designed to work alongside your medical care.

How long until I notice changes?

Some people notice shifts in a few weeks. Deeper patterns, such as autoimmune activity, are tracked over months.

Should I bring old labs?

Yes. Bring anything from the last two to three years. Trends are valuable.
