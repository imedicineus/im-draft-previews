2. What Qigong Is

Qigong combines slow movement, relaxed breathing and focused attention. It comes from Chinese medicine and the martial arts. Medical qigong is the branch focused on health and well being. There is no belief system to adopt and no special fitness level required.

5. The 70 Percent Rule

Work at about 70 percent of your effort and range. Never strain or hold your breath. Qigong is about relaxing tension, not adding to it.

Breath and the Nervous System

Slow, steady breathing with an extended exhale is associated with greater activity in the calming, parasympathetic branch of the nervous system. Qigong builds that breathing pattern into movement, which can make it easier to practice than seated breathing exercises for people who find stillness difficult.

Posture for Desk Workers

Many qigong postures emphasize a long spine, relaxed shoulders and a balanced head position. For people who spend hours looking at screens, these cues can serve as a daily reset.

Research Overview

Research on qigong and tai chi has grown over the past two decades. A Cochrane review evaluated these practices for certain conditions (PMC6360922), and other reviews have examined effects on stress, mood and quality of life (PMC3893407, PMC8504989). The NCCIH summarizes the evidence, noting possible benefits for balance and quality of life. Many studies are small, and findings vary.

Starting Safely

Check with your physician before starting any new exercise, especially with heart, balance or joint conditions. Begin with a few minutes a day, practice near a chair if balance is a concern and stop if you feel dizzy or unwell.

A One Minute Reset for the Office

Sit tall at the edge of your chair, feet flat. Rest your hands on your belly. Breathe in through the nose for a slow count of four, then out for a count of six. Let your shoulders drop with each exhale. Repeat six times.

Qigong for Focus

Many professionals practice qigong before a demanding day, describing it as a way to clear mental clutter. The slow, coordinated movements demand just enough attention to pull the mind away from the to do list without adding strain.

Three Foundational Movements

Lifting the sky. Hands float up in front of the body and overhead on the inhale, then sink down the sides on the exhale. This opens the chest and lengthens the spine.

Pushing the mountain. Palms press forward slowly as you exhale and draw back as you inhale. The shoulders stay low, and the movement comes from the legs and waist.

Turning the waist. With knees soft, the torso rotates gently left and right, letting the arms swing loosely. This mobilizes the spine and hips.

Each is simple to learn and safe for most people when kept within a comfortable range.

Qigong for Travelers

Hotel rooms, airport lounges and early mornings before a conference are all practical settings. A few minutes of standing practice can help counter long periods of sitting on flights. Dr. Fields can design a travel version of your routine that needs about a square yard of space.

Qigong and Leadership

Leaders often describe qigong as practice in staying calm under pressure. Learning to keep the breath slow while concentrating on a movement trains a skill that carries into tense meetings and negotiations. This is a personal observation many practitioners share, not a measured outcome, but it is one reason the practice appeals to executives.

How Long Before It Feels Natural?

Most students learn the basic sequence within a few sessions. It usually takes a few weeks of daily practice before the movements feel automatic and the breath coordinates without effort. Keep sessions short and consistent rather than long and occasional.

Standing Practice

One of the oldest forms of qigong is simply standing still in a relaxed posture, sometimes called standing like a tree. Feet are shoulder width apart, knees are slightly bent, arms are rounded in front of the chest as if holding a large ball and the breath is slow and natural. Beginners start with one or two minutes. It looks easy, but it teaches a great deal about where you hold tension. Over time, many students extend it to ten minutes or longer.

Qigong and Sleep

A short, gentle routine in the evening can be part of a wind down ritual. The slow pace and long exhales may help the transition from work mode to rest. Pair it with dim lights and a screen cutoff for the best chance of a restful night.

Combining Qigong With Treatment

When qigong is paired with acupuncture, herbal medicine or functional medicine, patients have something active to do every day. Dr. Fields often assigns one movement at a time so the habit builds steadily.

Seated Qigong

Not everyone can stand comfortably for long periods. Seated qigong adapts the arm movements, breathing and attention work to a chair. It is useful for people recovering from injury, those with balance concerns and anyone wanting a discreet practice at their desk. Sit near the front edge of the seat, keep both feet flat and let the spine lengthen upward without stiffness.

Tracking Your Practice

A simple calendar check mark each day you practice is enough. Some students also note their stress level before and after on a 1 to 10 scale. Watching those numbers over a month often motivates people more than any lecture could.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is qigong the same as tai chi?

They are related. Tai chi is a martial art practiced slowly, while qigong is a broader family of health exercises.

Do I need to be flexible?

No. Movements are adapted to your range.

Can I learn online?

Ask the office about current options.

When is the best time to practice?

Whatever time you will actually keep. Many people choose early morning.
