What Qigong Is

Qigong combines slow, flowing movement, relaxed breathing and calm attention. In Chinese medicine, it is described as a way to cultivate and circulate qi, the body's vital energy. In modern terms, it is gentle exercise that trains posture, balance and breathing while quieting a busy mind. You do not need to adopt any belief system to benefit.

03

What Drew Me to It

My training took me to the Shaolin Temple in China, where I studied and later led retreats. Practicing in that setting taught me that the gentlest movements can be the most profound. Qigong can be adapted for students of nearly any age, from teenagers to older adults.

04

What the Research Says

I want to be honest about the evidence. The NCCIH summarizes research suggesting tai chi and qigong may improve balance and reduce falls in older adults, and may help quality of life in some chronic conditions. Reviews have looked at blood pressure, mood, sleep and fatigue (PMC3893407, PMC5861462, PMC8504989). A Cochrane review examined these practices for specific conditions (PMC6360922), and other studies have explored chronic pain and stress (PMC8257957, PubMed 30125554). Many trials are small, so I describe qigong as promising and supportive rather than a treatment on its own.

A Routine to Begin With

Here is a short sequence I often give new students. Practice it slowly, breathing comfortably, and never push into pain.

Settle (one minute). Stand with feet hip width apart, knees soft, shoulders relaxed and the crown of the head lifting gently. Breathe slowly through the nose.

Raise the hands (eight times). As you inhale, let the hands float up in front of you to shoulder height. As you exhale, let them sink back down.

Open the chest (six times). Bring the hands together in front of the chest, then open them wide as you inhale, and bring them back as you exhale.

Turn the waist (ten times). Let the arms hang loosely and turn gently side to side, letting them swing like ribbons.

Shift the weight (ten times). With a chair beside you, slowly shift your weight from one foot to the other.

Gather and rest (one minute). Rest the hands over the lower belly and breathe quietly.

I also teach a longer sequence I call Nine Celestial Cycle qigong, about nine minutes long, which moves systematically from head to toe.

The Three Things I Watch For

Posture. Upright and relaxed, never rigid.

Breath. Slow, smooth and comfortable, never forced or held.

Attention. Resting on the movement, gently returning when it wanders.

Qigong for Different Seasons of Life

Students. A few minutes of breathing before study or exams can help focus.

Busy parents. A morning routine before the house wakes up, or a short practice after the kids are asleep.

Athletes. Better body awareness, balance and relaxed breathing.

Older adults. Balance training, gentle joint movement and calm. Seated versions work well.

How Qigong Fits With Treatment

I often recommend qigong alongside acupuncture, Chinese herbal medicine and functional medicine. In my experience, patients who practice at home tend to stay more engaged in their own care.

Staying Safe

Qigong is low risk. Stop if you feel dizzy, short of breath or have pain. If you have a heart condition, vertigo, recent surgery or severe osteoporosis, check with your doctor before starting.

When to Seek Medical Care First

Chest pain, shortness of breath, sudden weakness or numbness, a severe headache unlike any before, high fever or rapidly worsening symptoms need prompt medical care. Call 911 in an emergency.

Breathing as the Foundation

If you remember only one thing, remember the breath. Breathe in slowly through the nose, then breathe out a little longer than you breathed in. Keep it comfortable. Many people find that a few minutes of this alone helps them feel calmer. Never force the breath or hold it.

Making Practice a Habit

Choose a regular time, such as right after waking or before dinner. Start with five minutes. Keep a simple log of the days you practice and how you feel. Practicing with a spouse, friend or child makes it easier to keep going.

Practicing Outdoors in Parkland

Parkland's parks and quiet neighborhoods are lovely places to practice in the early morning or evening. In summer, choose shade, bring water and avoid the midday heat.

Common Beginner Mistakes

Locking the knees, raising the shoulders, holding the breath, moving too quickly and pushing into pain. I correct these early, because small adjustments make practice much more comfortable.

Tracking Your Progress

Note how long you can stand on one foot while lightly holding a counter, how easily you rise from a chair and how you rate your stress before and after practice. Small improvements over weeks are encouraging and worth celebrating.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I need to be flexible?

Not at all.

How often should I practice?

Ten minutes most days is a wonderful goal.

Can I practice sitting down?

Yes. Seated qigong is very effective for many people.

What is the difference between tai chi and qigong?

Tai chi is a martial art practiced slowly as moving meditation. Qigong is a broader family of health exercises.

Can my teenager practice with me?

Yes. Practicing together is a wonderful way to build the habit, and teens often enjoy the breathing work before exams or games.

Do I need any equipment?

No. Comfortable clothes, flat shoes or bare feet, and a sturdy chair nearby for balance are all you need.
