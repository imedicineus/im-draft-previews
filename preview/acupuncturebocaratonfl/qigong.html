Myth: Qigong Is Too Slow to Be Exercise

Fact: Slow is the point, and it is harder than it looks. Moving slowly through weight shifts and turns challenges balance and leg strength, and holding relaxed postures builds endurance in muscles that support the joints.

The deeper story

For readers who want the details: the research, the history and the reasoning behind how we work.

Balance and Fall Prevention

Falls are a serious concern as we age, and balance tends to decline without practice. Tai chi, qigong's close cousin, has been studied for fall prevention in older adults, with several trials suggesting fewer falls among participants. The NCCIH notes that tai chi may help improve balance and stability and reduce falls in older people. Qigong shares many of the same movement principles.

Joint Friendly Movement

Qigong keeps joints moving through comfortable ranges without impact. People with arthritis often find it a manageable form of activity. The emphasis on soft knees and relaxed alignment helps avoid strain.

Breath and Heart Rate

Slow breathing, especially with long exhales, is associated with increased parasympathetic activity, the rest and recover side of the nervous system. Qigong coordinates every movement with the breath, which makes slow breathing a natural part of practice.

What the Studies Say

Research on qigong and tai chi has grown substantially. Reviews have examined effects on balance, blood pressure, mood, sleep and quality of life (PMC3893407, PMC5861462), and a Cochrane review examined these practices for specific conditions (PMC6360922). Additional studies have looked at fatigue and well being (PMC8257957). Results are promising in some areas, but many studies are small and further research is needed.

Qigong for Golfers and Tennis Players

Rotation, balance and relaxed power are central to both qigong and racquet or club sports. Turning the waist while keeping the knees soft and the shoulders relaxed is a basic qigong skill and a basic swing skill. Some players find qigong a useful warm up.

Practice Outdoors

Boca Raton offers parks, beaches and shaded patios that make outdoor practice pleasant. Go early or late to avoid the strongest heat, drink water and move inside if storms approach.

Safety First

Check with your physician before starting, particularly with heart conditions, recent surgery, dizziness or significant balance problems. Practice near a chair or wall if you need support.

A Simple Balance Drill

Stand beside a counter. Shift your weight slowly to the right foot, lift the left heel, then lower it and shift back. Repeat ten times each side, breathing slowly. As it becomes easy, lift the toes slightly off the floor.

The Breath Ladder

Beginners often start with this exercise. Stand comfortably. Breathe in through the nose for a count of three, out for four. After a minute, extend to four in, five out. Then five in, six out. Never strain. If you feel lightheaded, return to normal breathing. This simple ladder teaches the slow, smooth breath that underlies all qigong.

Morning Versus Evening Practice

Morning practice tends to feel energizing and helps set posture for the day. Evening practice tends to feel calming and may support sleep. Dr. Fields may give you slightly different movements for each, emphasizing lifting and opening in the morning and sinking and settling at night.

Qigong on Recovery Days

After a hard match or a long walk, gentle qigong keeps blood moving without adding stress. Many active adults use a short routine as an active recovery session, combining it with stretching and hydration.

Practicing With a Partner

Some couples and friends practice together, which helps with accountability. Moving in sync with another person is also an enjoyable way to make the practice social. Group classes, when available, offer the same benefit.

Qigong and Acupuncture

Dr. Fields often pairs qigong instruction with acupuncture. In Chinese medicine theory, acupuncture adjusts the flow of qi from the outside, while qigong trains you to cultivate it from the inside. In practical terms, one is treatment and the other is self care, and they complement each other.

Five Minutes a Day

Research and experience both suggest that regular practice matters more than long sessions. Five focused minutes most days of the week is a realistic starting point. Many people practice right after brushing their teeth in the morning, tying the new habit to an existing one.

Qigong and Posture

Years of sitting, driving and looking at screens round the shoulders and push the head forward. Qigong cues such as "suspend the crown of the head" and "let the shoulders sink" counter those patterns gently every time you practice.

Common Beginner Mistakes

Locking the knees, lifting the shoulders, holding the breath and moving too fast are the four most common errors. Dr. Fields watches for each and offers simple cues to correct them.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much space do I need?

About the size of a yoga mat.

Can I practice with a knee replacement?

Often yes, with modifications. Check with your surgeon.

What should I wear?

Loose, breathable clothing and flat, flexible shoes, or bare feet when indoors on a safe surface.

How soon will I notice anything?

Many people feel calmer after a single session. Balance changes take weeks of regular practice.
