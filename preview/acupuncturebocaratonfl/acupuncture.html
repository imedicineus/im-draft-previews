Myth: The Needles Hurt

Fact: Acupuncture needles are hair thin, solid and flexible, nothing like the hollow needles used for shots or blood draws. Most people notice a brief pinch, then a heavy, warm or tingling sensation. Plenty of patients fall asleep on the table.

03

Myth: You Have to Believe in It

Fact: Clinical trials compare real acupuncture against sham versions and against usual care, which is how researchers try to separate the treatment effect from expectation. Some well designed research has found benefits beyond sham for certain chronic pain conditions. Belief is not a requirement. Showing up and giving it a fair trial is.

04

Myth: One Session Should Fix It

Fact: Acupuncture tends to work cumulatively. A typical course is weekly for several weeks, followed by a check on progress. Some people notice change early. Others need more time, especially when a problem has been around for years.

05

Myth: It Conflicts With Medical Care

Fact: Acupuncture is commonly used alongside conventional treatment, including physical therapy and medication. Keep your physicians in the loop, and Dr. Fields will do the same with your permission.

The deeper story

For readers who want the details: the research, the history and the reasoning behind how we work.

How Needles Change Pain Signals

When a needle is placed in muscle, it stimulates sensory nerve fibers. Those signals travel to the spinal cord and brain, where they can influence how pain is processed. Researchers have studied several possible mechanisms, including the release of the body's own pain relieving chemicals and changes in brain activity. The gate control theory, first proposed in 1965 (Melzack and Wall), offered an early model for how competing sensory input can dampen pain, and later work has expanded on it.

The Connective Tissue Angle

Fascia, the web of connective tissue surrounding muscles and organs, has drawn growing research attention. Studies have observed that rotating an acupuncture needle can wind connective tissue fibers around it, which may transmit a mechanical signal to surrounding cells (Langevin et al., 2001). Research published in 2018 described fluid filled spaces within connective tissue throughout the body (Benias et al.). These findings are intriguing, and scientists are still working out what they mean for acupuncture.

The Traditional Model

Chinese medicine describes channels or meridians that carry qi and blood. When flow is blocked, pain follows. When it is weak, fatigue and poor recovery follow. Points along these channels are selected to restore smooth flow. The language is ancient, but it remains a practical map for organizing a treatment, and Dr. Fields blends it with modern anatomy.

What the Research Says

An individual patient data meta-analysis of nearly 21,000 patients in randomized trials found that acupuncture outperformed both sham and no acupuncture controls for chronic musculoskeletal pain, headache and osteoarthritis pain, with effects that largely persisted at one year (Vickers et al., 2018). The NCCIH summarizes evidence for several pain conditions while noting that research continues. Individual results vary.

Knees: A Closer Look

Knee osteoarthritis is common among active adults and has been studied fairly extensively with acupuncture. Dr. Fields treats points around the knee and along related channels, then pairs treatment with strengthening for the quadriceps, hips and calves. Strong muscles absorb load that would otherwise land on the joint.

Shoulders and Overhead Sports

Swimming strokes, serves and overhead smashes all depend on a healthy rotator cuff and good shoulder blade control. When pain develops, Dr. Fields evaluates which movements trigger it and treats both the sore area and the muscles that should be sharing the load. Persistent weakness or night pain warrants an orthopedic evaluation, and he will recommend one if needed.

Feet and Ankles

Plantar fasciitis, old ankle sprains and neuropathy all affect how you walk, which in turn affects knees, hips and back. Treatment may include points on the foot and lower leg, calf work and footwear advice.

Recovery and Sleep

Recovery happens during sleep. If you are not sleeping, aches linger longer and energy drops. Acupuncture plans for active adults often include points traditionally used for calming and sleep, plus practical advice on evening routines.

Safety

Acupuncture by a licensed practitioner using sterile, single use needles has a low rate of serious side effects. Mild bruising, soreness or temporary fatigue may occur. Tell us if you take blood thinners, have a pacemaker, a bleeding disorder or are pregnant.

Related Therapies

Your plan may also include dry needling for trigger points, cupping or gua sha for tight tissue, Chinese herbal medicine or functional medicine for whole body support, and qigong for balance and daily movement.

Myth: Electroacupuncture Is a Shock

Fact: Electroacupuncture connects a small device to a pair of needles and delivers a gentle pulsing current. You feel a light tapping or buzzing, adjusted to a comfortable level. It is often used for muscle and nerve related pain and is skipped for people with pacemakers.

Staying Active Between Visits

The best results usually come when treatment supports activity rather than replacing it. Dr. Fields may suggest temporarily changing how often you play, swapping a high impact session for swimming or cycling, or adding mobility work. The goal is always to get you back to full activity, not to sideline you.

Myth: Acupuncture Is New Age

Fact: It is one of the oldest documented medical practices, and today it is studied in universities and offered in many hospital systems.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I play the day of treatment?

Light activity is usually fine. Save your hardest effort for the next day.

How many sessions will I need?

It depends on the problem and how long you have had it. Dr. Fields reassesses after the first few visits.

Is acupuncture safe if I have a joint replacement?

Generally yes, with appropriate precautions near the surgical site. Tell us about any implants.

Does Medicare cover acupuncture?

Medicare covers acupuncture for chronic low back pain under specific conditions and providers. Call to discuss your coverage.

What should I wear?

Loose clothing that rolls above the knees and elbows.
