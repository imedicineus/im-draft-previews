Acupuncture research: what it does and doesn't show

Where acupuncture evidence is good, mixed or thin.

  • 5 min read
  • Sources linked
  • Written by Dr. Fields, review pending
05

"Does acupuncture work?" is a fair question, and it has a more interesting answer than yes or no. For some problems the evidence is reasonably good. For others it is mixed or weak, and for a few it points the other way.

I would rather give you the real picture than a sales pitch. Most of what follows comes from the U.S. National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health (NCCIH), which summarizes the research without a stake in the outcome.

Why "prove" is the wrong word

Science rarely proves anything the way a math problem is proved. It builds a picture slowly, trial by trial, and it changes when better studies arrive.

So when someone says a study "proves" Chinese medicine works, treat it as marketing. A better question is what kind of evidence exists, how big and well-run the studies are, and how much benefit they found.

NCCIH's summary of traditional Chinese medicine is a good model of that tone. It says some approaches, such as acupuncture and tai chi, may help with quality of life and certain pain conditions, while studies of herbal products have had mixed results (NCCIH, Traditional Chinese Medicine).

Where the evidence is strongest: pain

Most Americans who try acupuncture do so for pain. NCCIH's review of the research says it may be helpful for several pain conditions, including back or neck pain, knee pain from osteoarthritis, and pain after surgery (NCCIH, Acupuncture: Effectiveness and Safety).

A few specifics from that review:

  • A 2018 review of 12 studies with 8,003 participants found acupuncture more effective than no treatment for back or neck pain, and a review of 10 studies with 1,963 participants found it more effective than sham acupuncture.
  • In both reviews the pain-relieving effect was comparable to that of nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs).
  • A 2017 guideline from the American College of Physicians listed acupuncture among the non-drug options recommended as first-line care for chronic low-back pain.
  • A 2019 guideline from the American College of Rheumatology and the Arthritis Foundation conditionally recommends it for osteoarthritis of the knee, hip or hand.

Notice the wording "conditionally" and "may." Experts choose those words on purpose.

The placebo question, honestly

Here is the part that sometimes gets glossed over. In many trials, real acupuncture beat no treatment by a wide margin, but beat sham acupuncture, where needles are fake or placed in non-points, by a smaller one.

NCCIH explains that this suggests nonspecific effects, such as belief in the treatment and the relationship with the practitioner, contribute to the benefit. That does not mean the relief is imaginary. Pain is partly a brain-and-expectation experience, and a treatment that reduces your pain still reduces your pain.

But it does mean people should be wary of claims that depend on a specific mechanism, like "this needle unblocks that organ." The science does not support that level of precision yet.

Where results are mixed

Plenty of conditions fall in the gray middle. A few examples from the NCCIH summary:

  • Irritable bowel syndrome: a 2019 review of 41 studies found acupuncture was no more effective than sham, though there was some evidence it could help when added to other treatments.
  • Hot flashes in menopause: acupuncture was better than no acupuncture at reducing frequency and severity, but was not shown to be better than sham.
  • Depression: a 2018 review found acupuncture may moderately reduce severity, but most of the studies were low or very low quality.
  • In vitro fertilization: a 2021 review of six studies found no difference in pregnancy or live birth rates between acupuncture and sham.

That last one is worth sitting with. If someone is selling acupuncture as a way to improve IVF success, the best current review does not support that claim.

Where there is promising but limited evidence

Some findings are encouraging yet not settled. NCCIH notes evidence that acupuncture may help with seasonal allergy symptoms and stress incontinence in women.

The incontinence result came from a rigorous study of about 500 women using electroacupuncture, in which about two-thirds had a drop in leakage of 50 percent or more.

How to judge a claim yourself

You do not need a science degree to ask good questions. A few habits go a long way.

First, look for how many people were studied. A trial of 20 people can be interesting, but it cannot settle a debate.

Second, look for a comparison group, ideally one that got a sham version. Third, notice who is talking. A clinic describing its own results is not the same as an independent review, and a testimonial is not a trial.

Finally, watch for words like "cure," "reverse" and "guaranteed." Real evidence rarely uses them.

Safety

NCCIH says relatively few complications have been reported, but problems have come from nonsterile needles and improper delivery. In the United States the FDA regulates acupuncture needles as medical devices and requires that they be sterile and single-use.

So the safety question is mostly about who is doing it. Look for a licensed practitioner, single-use needles, and someone who tells you plainly what they do and do not know. NCCIH also advises that people should not use acupuncture to postpone seeing a health care provider about a health problem.

What the clinics in this network say

If you want a local viewpoint after reading the research, there are a few places to start. Vibrant Health Center in Fort Lauderdale has an article on how acupuncture helps anxiety and what the research shows.

Integrative Medicine US has a piece on what to expect from acupuncture for back pain, and a Fort Lauderdale practice describes its approach to acupuncture for pain relief. Read them with the questions above in mind, and see whether they hold to the same standard.

The takeaway

Acupuncture has respectable evidence for several kinds of pain, modest or unclear evidence for many other things, and a good safety record in trained hands. It is a reasonable option to discuss with your clinician, not a replacement for one.

If you are curious, try it with realistic expectations. Give it a handful of visits, track how you feel, and stop if it is not helping.
