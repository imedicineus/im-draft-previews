Acupressure at home: a careful beginner's guide

Four points to try, how hard to press, and when to skip it.

  • 5 min read
  • Sources linked
  • Reviewed by Dr. Fields (pending)

You have probably done a version of acupressure without calling it that. Rubbing your temples during a headache or pressing the muscle between thumb and finger counts as the same family of habit.

Acupressure is the needle-free cousin of acupuncture. It uses finger or thumb pressure on specific points that traditional Chinese medicine has mapped over centuries.

Let me be plain about what this guide is. It is a low-risk self-care tool to try, with modest evidence behind some uses and thin evidence behind others.

What acupressure is, in everyday terms

In traditional Chinese medicine, the body has pathways called meridians, and points along them are called acupoints. The theory says that steady pressure on these points helps energy, or qi, move more freely.

You do not need to believe the theory for the practice to feel good. Firm, slow pressure on tense tissue is relaxing, and that is a reasonable thing to want.

Cleveland Clinic describes acupressure as a form of massage you can do on yourself, and notes that research on its effectiveness is still ongoing (Cleveland Clinic, What Is Acupressure?). That caution is worth keeping in your head as you read the rest.

What the research does and does not show

The evidence is mixed, and it depends a lot on what you are trying to ease.

The strongest-looking work involves nausea. A recent Cochrane review of wrist stimulation at the point called PC6 looked at its use for nausea after surgery, and found it may reduce nausea and vomiting compared with a sham version, though with low confidence in the result (Cochrane network meta-analysis).

For nausea in early pregnancy, the picture is weaker. Cochrane's review of interventions found limited and inconsistent evidence, and no clear benefit for wrist acupressure (Cochrane, nausea and vomiting in early pregnancy).

For stress, sleep and tension, many trials are small or loosely designed. So the reasonable stance is curiosity. Try it, pay attention, and judge by how you feel, without expecting a guarantee.

Where people usually start

These are common beginner points. None of them is magic, and the exact location can vary a little from person to person.

PC6, on the inner forearm

Turn your palm up. Measure about three finger-widths up from the wrist crease, between the two big tendons in the middle of your forearm. This is the point used in motion-sickness wristbands.

LI4, between thumb and finger

Find the fleshy web between your thumb and index finger. Press at the highest point of the muscle when you squeeze them together. Avoid this point if you are pregnant, since traditional teaching treats it with caution.

LI20, beside the nostrils

These points sit at the creases beside each nostril. Cleveland Clinic's practitioner suggests gentle pressure here for stuffy sinuses.

Neck and shoulder tension

Many people simply press into the tight muscle at the base of the skull, or along the top of the shoulder where it feels tender. The classic named point is GB21, halfway between the neck and the edge of the shoulder.

How to press

Use your thumb or a knuckle. Press firmly enough to feel a dull ache or pressure, never sharp pain.

Hold for about a minute while you breathe slowly. Small circles are fine. Then let go gradually instead of pulling off fast.

Go through one or two points rather than ten. If you feel better, you will know which one helped, and that is more useful than a long routine you will not repeat.

Acupressure for travel

Travel is the most popular use. Many people press PC6 or wear a band that does it for them before a boat ride or long drive.

The research on motion sickness is limited, so treat this as something worth trying with no downside. Start before you feel sick, eat something light, and sit where the motion is smallest. Fresh air and looking at the horizon still help more than any wristband.

Safety and when to skip it

Acupressure is generally safe when pressure is moderate. A few situations call for caution.

  • Skip any area with broken, infected, bruised or swollen skin.
  • Avoid pressing over a tumor, a blood clot, or an area of recent surgery.
  • Check with your clinician first if you are pregnant, since some points are traditionally avoided.
  • Stop if you feel sharp pain, numbness, or dizziness.

If you take blood thinners or have a bleeding disorder, use light pressure and ask your care team. Bruising is the common side effect, and it is easier on people who bruise easily to press gently.

Nothing here should replace a visit for pain that is severe, sudden or getting worse. Chest pain, new weakness, or a headache unlike any you have had before needs a clinician today.

How it compares to acupuncture

Acupuncture and acupressure use many of the same points, but they are not the same thing. Acupuncture uses fine needles and, according to the U.S. National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health, has a larger research base, particularly for pain conditions such as low back pain, neck pain and knee osteoarthritis (NCCIH, Acupuncture: Effectiveness and Safety).

Acupressure is the piece you can take home. Many practitioners teach a few points for use between visits, and some patients find that is the part they keep doing for years.

If your neck or back is the issue, you may want to look at the clinic's pages on neck pain acupuncture in Coral Springs and a Fort Lauderdale explainer on how acupuncture is used for pain. Both describe how a practitioner approaches the problem.

A five-minute routine

Sit in a chair with your feet flat. Take three slow breaths.

Press PC6 on one wrist for one minute, then switch. Move to the muscle at the base of your skull and press upward for a minute. Finish by resting your hands in your lap and breathing for another minute.

That is it. Try it for a week and notice whether your evenings feel any different.
