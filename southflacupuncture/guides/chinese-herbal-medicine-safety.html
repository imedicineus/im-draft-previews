Chinese herbal medicine: basics and safety questions

Drug interactions, product quality and the questions to ask first.

  • 5 min read
  • Sources linked
  • Written by Dr. Fields, review pending

Herbs are the part of Chinese medicine that people understand least and ask about most. They also carry the most practical risk, which is why this article spends more time on the safety questions than on the sales pitch.

If you take any prescription medication, this is the page to read before you buy a bottle from a shelf or a website.

What Chinese herbal medicine is

Traditional Chinese medicine has used plant, mineral and occasionally animal materials for a very long time. Practitioners write formulas, which are combinations of several ingredients meant to work together and to balance each other.

That is a major difference from the single-herb supplements you see in a pharmacy aisle. A traditional formula might have eight to a dozen ingredients, with one or two chosen as the main actors and the others added to support them or soften side effects.

Formulas are also meant to be adjusted. A practitioner may change the recipe from visit to visit as your symptoms change.

What the evidence says

The National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health (NCCIH) is direct about this. Chinese herbal products have been studied for a wide range of serious conditions, but because many studies were of poor quality, no firm conclusions can be made about how well they work (NCCIH, Traditional Chinese Medicine: What You Need To Know).

Results have been mixed. Some trials look promising and some do not, and quality varies a lot.

That is not the same as saying herbs do nothing, but it does mean nobody can fairly promise results from herbal medicine in general. Herbs are a reason for more conversation with your own doctor, never a replacement for medical care.

This is also true of single herbs. For example, NCCIH's review of Asian ginseng describes a small amount of research on fatigue and thinking skills, with benefits that are small, inconsistent, or limited to certain groups (NCCIH, Herb-Drug Interactions: What the Science Says).

The safety problems worth knowing about

Plants are chemistry, and chemistry has side effects. Here is what the NCCIH and other health agencies flag.

Product quality

Some Chinese herbal products have been found to contain undeclared plant or animal material, drugs such as warfarin or diclofenac, heavy metals like arsenic, lead and cadmium, pesticides, or the wrong herb altogether. Mix-ups in which one herb was mistakenly swapped for another have caused serious harm, including organ damage.

The U.S. Food and Drug Administration oversees dietary supplements differently from drugs. Supplements are not approved by the FDA before they go on sale, which puts more responsibility on the maker and on you (FDA, Dietary Supplements).

Drug interactions

Herbs can change how your medications work. NCCIH's herb-drug interaction digest says that taking ginkgo with warfarin is linked to a higher risk of major bleeding, and that Asian ginseng might interact with some blood pressure drugs, statins and certain antidepressants.

St. John's wort, a European herb that often turns up in supplement blends, is another well-documented example, with interactions involving oral contraceptives, warfarin and some antidepressants. If you take any of those, a casual herb habit is not casual.

Pregnancy, children and surgery

Some herbs are considered unsafe in pregnancy, and others have little safety data. NCCIH advises that if you are pregnant or nursing, or thinking of using TCM for a child, you should be especially sure to talk with the relevant health care provider first.

Tell your surgeon and anesthesiologist about every herb you take, because some affect bleeding.

Questions to ask before you start

You are allowed to ask all of these, and a good practitioner will be glad you did.

  • What is in this formula, and can I see the ingredient list?
  • Where does the company source its herbs, and does it test for contaminants and for undeclared drugs?
  • Could this interact with my medications? Have you reviewed my full list?
  • How long should I take it, and what should I watch for?
  • When would you want me to stop and call you?

Bring your actual pill bottles or a photo of the labels. A memory of "the blue one for blood pressure" is not enough.

Who should prescribe

NCCIH recommends asking about the training and experience of any practitioner you consider. Most states regulate acupuncture practice, and most require certification from the National Certification Commission for Acupuncture and Oriental Medicine. NCCIH's page on credentials and licensing explains how to check.

A practitioner who also communicates with your primary care doctor is worth more than one who works in isolation. If you take multiple medications, that coordination is the safety feature.

What a good first herbal visit looks like

Expect questions about your health history, your digestion, your sleep, your energy and your medications. Expect to hear what the practitioner thinks the formula is meant to do, and what is not known.

Expect to leave with a written ingredient list. If someone sells you an unlabeled powder from a jar and cannot tell you what is in it, leave.

The short version

Chinese herbal medicine is a long tradition with mixed modern evidence and some real risks, especially around product quality and drug interactions. Ask for the ingredient list, bring your medication list, and use a licensed practitioner who is willing to work with your doctor.

Never stop a prescribed medication because of an herb. If you notice a rash, yellowing skin, dark urine, severe stomach upset, or unusual bleeding, stop the product and get medical help.

This article is educational and is not medical advice. Talk with your own clinician or pharmacist before starting any herb or supplement.
