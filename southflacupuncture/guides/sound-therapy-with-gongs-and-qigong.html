Gong sound therapy and qigong: what a session is like

What a gong session feels like and what the small research base says.

  • 6 min read
  • Sources linked
  • Written by Dr. Fields, review pending

A gong does something odd to a quiet room. The first strike is loud, then it blooms into a long wash of overtones that seems to fill the space and then slowly drain out of it. People often describe the silence afterward as the best part.

That is about as far as I can go on the experience itself, because it is different for everyone. Some people feel their shoulders drop within a minute. Others spend the whole session mentally rewriting a grocery list, and that is fine too.

What gong sound therapy actually is

Gong sound therapy, often called a sound bath, is a listening practice. You lie down or sit comfortably while a facilitator plays one or more gongs, and sometimes singing bowls or chimes, for roughly 30 to 60 minutes. There is nothing to do except breathe and let your attention wander.

It is not a medical treatment. No one is diagnosing anything, and no one should tell you it fixes a condition. Think of it as structured quiet with a very big instrument.

The idea of using sound for calm is old and shows up in many cultures. In Chinese medicine settings it sits alongside qigong and meditation as a way to settle the mind before or after other care. That pairing is a tradition and a practical habit, not a proven protocol.

What the research says (and what it does not)

Here is the honest state of things. There is a small amount of research, mostly on singing bowls rather than gongs, and most of it is early.

One observational study followed 62 adults who attended a singing bowl meditation. Participants reported less tension, anger, fatigue, anxiety and depressed mood afterward, and more spiritual well-being (Goldsby and colleagues, 2017, PubMed Central). People who were new to the practice reported a bigger drop in tension than experienced ones.

There was no control group, though. Nobody can say from that study whether the sound did the work, or whether lying down in a calm room for an hour would have done the same thing. It tells us the practice is easy to do and that people feel better right afterward, and it stops there.

So if someone promises that gongs will cure insomnia or reverse a disease, walk away. A fair claim sounds more like this: many people find a sound session relaxing, and relaxation is worth having.

Where qigong fits

Qigong (say "chee gong") is a Chinese mind-body practice that combines slow breathing, gentle movement and focused attention. The National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health describes it as part of traditional Chinese medicine, with forms that range from active movement to seated, nearly motionless meditation (NCCIH, Qigong: What You Need To Know).

Some qigong forms add sound. The breath may shift from belly breathing to breathing paired with spoken or hummed sounds, which is one reason qigong and sound work get grouped together. If you like the vibration of a gong, a humming breath exercise is a free version you can do in a parked car.

The research on qigong is broader than the research on sound baths, and it is still mixed. NCCIH notes that qigong appears to be safe, with few reported side effects, and that evidence for pain is conflicting. Some small studies in fibromyalgia found benefit, and the agency is careful to call that early.

For a close look at qigong classes and a seminar schedule, see the qi gong seminars page at Integrative Medicine US. The clinic in Coral Springs also describes how it uses the practice in care on its medical qi gong page.

What to expect at your first session

Most sessions follow a loose pattern. You arrive, find a spot on a mat, and get a blanket or a bolster if you want one. The facilitator may say a few words and then start playing softly.

A few practical points help.

  • Wear something you could nap in.
  • Eat lightly beforehand, because a growling stomach is a distraction in a quiet room.
  • Tell the facilitator if you are sensitive to loud sound, have tinnitus, or have a history of seizures or migraines triggered by sensory input.
  • Ask if you are pregnant or have a pacemaker, since you may want a clinician's opinion before lying close to a large vibrating instrument.

You might fall asleep. You might feel a little emotional, or a little silly. Both are normal, and neither means you did it wrong.

Who should check first

Sound sessions are low risk for most people, yet a few groups should talk to their own clinician first. That includes anyone with a seizure disorder, significant hearing problems, or a mental health condition where sudden emotional release could be hard to handle.

If you are in treatment for anxiety or depression, a sound bath is a nice extra at most. It is not a replacement for your care team. Tell them what you are trying so they have the full picture.

Qigong carries its own small caution. NCCIH notes that people who are pregnant should check with their provider, because some movements may need changing.

How to try it near you

You do not need to travel far. Look for a facilitator who is happy to explain what the session will involve, who does not make medical promises, and who lets you opt out of anything that feels uncomfortable.

There are Chinese medicine centers across Broward and Palm Beach counties that run qigong and meditation events. The Boca Raton group publishes a page on the six healing sounds, a classic qigong breathing exercise, plus a general overview of its medical qi gong classes. Both are good reading even if you never attend.

Short educational clips from Dr. Fields are also on TikTok, if you would rather watch a breathing exercise than read about one.

A simple practice you can do tonight

Sit or lie down somewhere you will not be interrupted. Put on a single low, sustained sound if you like, such as a recorded gong or just a fan.

Breathe in through your nose for about four counts. Let the breath out slowly through your mouth with a quiet hum, as long as feels comfortable. Do that for five minutes and then sit still for two more.

That is a small experiment, and it is enough. If it helps you sleep or feel calmer, keep it. If it does nothing, you lost seven minutes and gained a quiet room.

The short version

Gong sound therapy is a calm, low-effort listening practice with early, modest research behind it. Qigong has a wider evidence base and a good safety record, though the results for many conditions are still mixed. Try either for the experience, stay skeptical of big promises, and keep your regular medical care in place.
