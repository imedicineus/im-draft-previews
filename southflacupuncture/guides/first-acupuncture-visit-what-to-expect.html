Your first acupuncture visit: what to expect and ask

What happens, what the needles feel like, what to ask.

  • 5 min read
  • Sources linked
  • Written by Dr. Fields, review pending

A first appointment is mostly a conversation. If you have never tried acupuncture, the unknowns are usually the needles, the cost, and whether you are going to feel silly. Let me take them one at a time.

The short answer is that it is a quiet, fairly ordinary appointment. Most people are surprised by how little happens that feels dramatic.

Before you go

Eat something light an hour or two before. An empty stomach or a heavy meal both make people feel off on the table.

Wear loose clothing that rolls up past the knee and elbow. Bring a list of your medications and supplements, any recent lab results, and a short list of what you want help with.

Write down your top two or three goals. Practitioners can work with "my neck hurts when I turn left" much more easily than with "I want to feel better."

Finding a practitioner you can trust

Most states regulate acupuncture, and the rules differ. NCCIH advises asking about a practitioner's training and experience, and its page on credentials, licensing and education explains how to check.

Beyond the license, look for someone who explains what they are doing without making promises. If a clinic guarantees a cure, or pressures you to prepay for a large package on day one, take a step back.

It is also fair to ask who will actually be treating you, since some clinics have several practitioners.

What happens at the appointment

Expect paperwork first, then a conversation. A practitioner will ask about your main concern, your health history, your sleep, digestion, energy, stress and medications.

Many traditional Chinese medicine practitioners also look at your tongue and feel your pulse at the wrist. This can seem unusual if you have never seen it done. It is part of how they gather information in this system.

After that, you will be asked to lie down, face up or face down, depending on where the points are. The practitioner will explain what they plan to do. That is the time to say, "I am nervous about needles," because they can adjust.

What the needles feel like

Acupuncture needles are much thinner than the ones used for injections or blood draws. Many people feel a quick pinch or nothing at all as one goes in.

Then you may feel a dull ache, warmth, heaviness or tingling around the point. Practitioners often call this "de qi." If a sensation is sharp or uncomfortable, say so and the needle can be moved.

Most sessions use somewhere from a handful of needles to a couple of dozen. Needles often stay in place for roughly 15 to 30 minutes, though practices differ, so ask what to expect at yours.

Safety and cleanliness

According to NCCIH, relatively few complications are reported, but they have happened with nonsterile needles and improper technique (NCCIH, Acupuncture: Effectiveness and Safety). The FDA regulates acupuncture needles as medical devices and requires that they be sterile and labeled for single use.

You can simply ask whether the needles are single-use and whether they are opened in front of you. A good clinic will answer without hesitation.

Tell your practitioner if you take blood thinners, have a bleeding disorder, have a pacemaker, are pregnant or might be, or have an implanted electrical device. These affect how care is planned.

What you might feel afterward

Some people feel relaxed or sleepy. Some feel energized, and some feel nothing in particular. All are common.

Minor side effects can include a little soreness, a small bruise at a needle site, or lightheadedness when you stand up too fast. Take your time getting up, drink some water, and avoid scheduling a big meeting right after your first visit if you can.

Contact the clinic if something feels wrong, and seek urgent medical care for severe pain, trouble breathing, or signs of infection such as spreading redness, warmth or fever.

How many visits and what it costs

This varies, and no one can honestly promise an exact number before meeting you. Many practitioners suggest a short series of visits close together at the start, then spacing them out if things improve.

A fair clinic can tell you at the first appointment what they recommend and why, and will reassess as you go. Ask about the cost per visit and whether they take insurance. Some policies cover acupuncture and others do not, and Medicare covers it only for chronic low-back pain (NCCIH, linked above).

Keep a simple note after each visit. A one-line entry about pain, sleep, and mood makes it much easier to tell whether this is helping.

Questions worth asking

  • What is your license and where did you train?
  • What do you think is going on in my case, and what is the plan?
  • How will we decide whether this is working?
  • What are the costs, and what is the cancellation policy?
  • Will you coordinate with my other providers?

You do not need to ask all of them. Pick the two that matter most to you.

The short version

Show up fed and dressed comfortably, bring your medication list, and ask your questions. Expect a conversation, a few thin needles, and a quiet half hour.

If anything about a clinic feels pushy or vague, you are free to leave and try somewhere else. Good practitioners want you informed.
